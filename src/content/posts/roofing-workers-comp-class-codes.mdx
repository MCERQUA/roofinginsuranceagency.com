## Why Roofing Workers' Comp Classification Is High-Stakes

Roofing is one of the highest-hazard trades in construction. Workers' comp rates for roofing labor reflect that — and the difference between the right class code and the wrong one can mean thousands of dollars per year in premium, plus significant exposure at audit.

Getting classification right isn't just about saving money. Wrong codes can mean you're actually under-insured for the real risks your crews face.

## The Primary Roofing Class Codes

Workers' comp classification codes vary by state (some states use NCCI codes, others use their own bureau), but the most common codes for roofing work are:

**Code 5551 — Roofing: All Kinds**
This is the standard code for roofing installation work — applying shingles, tile, metal panels, modified bitumen, TPO membranes, or other roofing materials to residential or commercial structures. It's a high-rate code because the fall exposure is real and significant.

**Code 5474 — Plastering & Stucco**
Sometimes incorrectly applied to certain exterior coating applications. Make sure your crews doing actual roofing aren't being misclassified here, which could result in coverage gaps at claim time.

**Code 8227 — Roofing Contractors — Supervisors**
Some states separate supervisory staff (project managers, foremen who primarily supervise without regular roofing work) from field labor. If applicable in your state, this can reduce premium on supervisor payroll.

**Code 8742 — Outside Sales**
If you have estimators or sales representatives who primarily work in the field without doing roofing labor, they may qualify for a lower-rate sales code. This varies by state and by how much field time these employees actually spend.

## The Audit Surprise Problem

Workers' comp policies are written on estimated payroll at the start of the policy year. At the end of the year, the carrier audits your actual payroll — and adjusts the premium up or down.

Common audit triggers for roofing contractors:

- **Misclassified payroll** — If office staff is incorrectly carried under a roofing code, you're overpaying. Conversely, if roofing field labor is carried under a clerical code, you're underinsured and will face a large audit bill.
- **Subcontractor payroll** — If you use subcontractors who don't have their own workers' comp, their payroll may get included in your audit as if they were employees. Always collect certificates from subs.
- **Undisclosed work types** — If your crews take on roofing repair, tear-off, or installation of a different roofing type than what was disclosed at binding, the carrier may reclassify or surcharge at audit.

## Why Subcontractor Certificates Matter

If you use subcontractors without their own workers' comp coverage, your carrier may treat their payroll as your payroll at audit — and rate it at your roofing class code rate. This can produce a large, unexpected audit bill.

The fix is simple: require certificates of insurance from every subcontractor showing current workers' comp, and keep them on file. If a sub is out of coverage during the period they work for you, your audit exposure is real.

## Correctly Classifying Multi-Trade Operations

Some roofing contractors also perform related work — gutters, siding, insulation, or solar installations. Each trade type has its own class code and rate. If your policy is classified only under roofing code 5551, but your crews are doing solar installation (Code 5540) or siding (Code 5645), you need those codes added to your policy.

Operating under a single roofing code when you perform multiple trade types can mean undercoverage for workers injured while doing the non-roofing work.

## What to Bring to a Workers' Comp Quote

To get an accurate workers' comp quote — and avoid audit surprises — have ready:

- Total payroll by job type (field roofing, field supervisors, estimators/sales, office/admin)
- List of subcontractors and whether they carry their own workers' comp
- States where your crews work (multi-state operations need coverage in each state)
- Any experience modification factor (X-mod) from prior coverage
- Loss run for the last 3–5 years

Accurate information at binding means accurate classification — and no audit shock at the end of the year.
